02  HOW DIEGETIC IS IT

The test applied to every element: could a machinist have made this, and would an operator know what it does without being told? At the first audit the board failed that test in five places, all of them moments where it explained itself rather than showed itself. Those are now closed, and the remaining gap is a different kind — not software tells, but systems that model their own domain and reach nothing outside it.

What carries the fiction. The arming ladders are the strongest work: booted rockers thrown in order, each stating its own interlock, ending in a lever or a guarded press. The rotary selectors have detents and their pointer snaps between real positions. The power buses fill one unit at a time, so spinning up a bus takes visible time. The periscope descends from a carriage that stays visible when stowed, and that stub is its own control — no icon, no button, just the object. The sensor loop rewards understanding the equipment rather than finding the right tab.

What was fixed. Six drawers carried a × close glyph; they now carry machined latches, and the rail key that opened a drawer is what stows it. The helm printed CLICK TO OPEN and CLICK TO STOW on its face; it now has a latch that shows its own position, thrown left when stowed and right when out. The heading wheel was captioned A / D, which was keyboard help engraved onto hardware. And eleven rail keys were labelled only by hover tooltip — each now has an engraved aluminium legend plate, which was the largest single diegetic gap and is also the change that most improved ordinary use.

What is still not diegetic, by decision. Drawers slide in from beyond the right edge with no slot or housing on the frame to emerge from (D-05, deferred). The alarm strip still says CLICK TO ACKNOWLEDGE, kept deliberately because an alarm that cannot be silenced is worse than one that names the gesture.

03  COLOUR

The rule is that accent colour carries meaning only. At the first audit the board broke it in four ways. All four are now closed, and two of them closed by amending the rules rather than the build — the build had learned something the document never recorded.

Rail glyphs keep their house colour — by decision. The six systems-rail glyphs are coloured by the system they open: red fire control, blue sensors, hangar and comms, amber engineering, green reactor. This is a deliberate exception to the neutrality rule, taken on gameplay grounds: the rail is the board’s primary navigation and an operator needs to find a system without reading eleven 7px legends. Colour-coded navigation is a video-game affordance and it wins here. The exception is bounded — it applies to rail glyphs only, one colour per key, and each key’s drawer-out state is carried by a separate lamp rather than by the glyph.

Decorative washes, removed. Five of the six drawer headers carried a meaning-colour wash — power and sensors in nominal green, comms and hangar in own-force blue, fire control in hostile red. That was the meaning palette used as branding across most of the board. Every header is now neutral; no panel has a house colour and no drawer is tinted by the system it serves.

Green was doing six jobs; now it does four. Nominal, DRAW, friendly, and selected. The drawer washes are gone. Phosphor was the interesting case: the nav wheel and the scan cone glow green because the tube is made of phosphor, the same way the frame is grey because it is steel. That is a material property, not a state, and the rules now say so explicitly — it does not count against the one-accent-per-element limit.

Amber was doing five jobs; now it does four. Standby, caution, resolving, and alert condition. The fire-control and periscope treatment was thematic rather than semantic and covered a large area of the board: the mount and warhead selectors, the corridor reticle, the bearing readout, the target designation, the zoom keys, the stub bezel. All of it neutralised. A choice between mounts or warheads is not a state, and a sight’s own reticle is etched glass; both now read in data ink. ON BEARING went green, because being on bearing is nominal rather than a caution.

Four ambers became one. Alongside the sanctioned #E0A020 the board carried three near-duplicates — #E8A33D on the lock bracket and bearing echo, #E0A33D on the PDC arc, #F0A020 on paused and slewing. All meant resolving or standby, so all folded. One marker carried two of them at once, breaking the one-accent-per-element rule on a single 15px square. The fold took two passes because the first matched on hex spelling and missed every rgba() form of the same colours — the near-duplicate call-site failure §08 records. Verified afterwards by walking computed styles in the DOM for amber-range triplets: one distinct amber, 153 uses.

The off-palette purple, removed at its root. The UV spectrum was #9A72D8, outside the sanctioned four. Examined properly, the purple was a symptom: the four spectra were amber, green, purple and blue, so three of them were meaning colours already doing other jobs, and the passive bearing lines carried that collision onto the plot. Rather than declare a fifth accent, hue came off the selector entirely — a spectrum band is which channel you are listening on, not a state, and selection is carried by ink value, ground and lamp. Bearing lines now take the grid tint, since a bearing means the same thing whichever band heard it.

The stencil lift, finished. When the section grounds went from black to machined metal the low-value inks failed contrast at 7px and were lifted a step — but that lift was a string replace over the markup only, so twenty-five state-dependent colours in the logic class still emitted pre-lift inks onto the lightened grounds. A live legibility failure on six panels, since fixed. The cause is worth keeping: a colour change scoped to the markup silently misses every colour computed from state.

One judgement recorded. Roughly seventeen bright hex values remain that are not literal palette entries — #B2281A and #7A1509 on the red rocker paddles, #FFC44D on the reactor’s upper segments. These are light and shade stops within one sanctioned hue, which a bevelled object needs. The rule is one accent per element, not one hex.

The material conflict, resolved. The frame is a genuine brushed-metal shader; the drawers were a two-stop CSS gradient with a third of its value range and no surface detail, so they read as printed rather than machined. They are now painted castings in their own right — matte body, a broad soft sheen, fine tooling grain on the same 158° axis as the frame light, and the arris worn back to bare metal along the top and left edges where a real casting rubs. Every plate and control face was regrained to match. Deliberately a different material from the frame now, rather than an accidental one.

Light direction is now declared, and mostly honoured. The rules state one direction: 158°, upper-left, every bevel lighting its top edge and shading its bottom. The build carries 118 gradients on that axis. It also carries 107 running at 180° — but those are overwhelmingly vertical fills inside recessed channels, where a straight-down gradient is what a lit track in a slot actually looks like, so they are not violations. The one genuine exception is the plot vignette, which implies light from the centre of the table; that is the tube’s own falloff rather than a lit surface, and the rules name it as the single exception.

05  LAYOUT

The stated layout rules are all met: rails middle-left and middle-right at identical pitch, drawers middle-right and mutually exclusive, periscope top centre, helm bottom centre protruding into the plot, nothing floating. The plot is the hero by area and now by value as well, since the grid brightening.

Three things were raised here on diegetic grounds rather than rule compliance. Two were settled by deciding the rule was wrong, which is worth recording as such rather than as a fix.

The top-left station, rebuilt. The finding as first written was wrong: heading and thrust were already at the helm, and only hull integrity sat at top left. The real fault was one housing doing three jobs — nameplate, damage readout and simulation control — with a hull percentage that came from nowhere and duplicated what engineering already owned. The time control is now its own recessed plate, visibly not ship hardware, and the invented hull figure is gone. A separate ship readout was tried four times and abandoned: a painted panel that floated, a plate that read as cheap beside the frame, and two hull drawings that did not match the vessel on the plot. The board did not need it.

Graticule labels stay on the glass — declined. The objection was that axis titles and tick numbers are permanent furniture and belong engraved on the bezel, as they would on real equipment. Declined on gameplay grounds: because the grid rescales with zoom, those numbers change, and watching them climb as you pull back is how the operator feels the vastness of the volume. A fixed engraved scale would read as more authentic hardware and communicate less. The live numbers earn their place on the phosphor.

Zoom does not magnify — closed by decision. The scale runs 1.0× down to 0.1×, so 1.0× is the closest view. 1× is the authored scale of the table and it is not meant to magnify past it; an operator wanting detail opens the sight, not a tighter plot.

06  HOW THE SYSTEMS RELATE

This was the most important finding of the first audit and the most uncomfortable: the systems were individually deep and almost entirely uncoupled. Each drawer modelled its own domain honestly and almost none of it reached across. Eleven of the thirteen relationships below are now wired; two are deferred by decision.

Power became the spine. It named sensors, weapons and engines as consumers and enforced none of them, and its fourth bus had no consumer at all. Now the reactor makes ten units and every consumer is capped by what its bus actually carries: sensor gain by SEN, thrust by ENG, the arming ladder and shot charge by WEP, engineering orders by AUX. That converted the unit economy from a display into a decision — allocating to weapons is thrust you do not have.

Damage rides the same spine. A compartment governs the system housed inside it, and a vented compartment counts as integrity zero because nobody can crew it. Reactor damage caps units; the magazine gates the arming ladder; the hangar gates launch; the engine room scales thrust; the bridge gates the firing solution. Alert condition is a permission gate rather than a mood: it sets crew at stations, releases weapons and flight operations, and doubles the repair rate at Condition 1.

Two controls used to lie. Active scan read EMITS — REVEALS SHIP and nothing was revealed; the sensor panel read NEEDS 2 UNITS ON SEN and took the gain regardless. Both now deliver what they state. In a diegetic interface a control that names a consequence and does not produce it is worse than one that says nothing.

The sensor loop was the exception at the first audit and is worth naming as the template the rest followed: passive gives bearing, the optic gives class, the ping gives range, and each fills a different column of the same contact board. No channel is redundant and none is sufficient. Every coupling added since was built to that shape — one system supplying something another cannot get for itself.

08  WHAT THE PASSES TAUGHT

This section replaces the original priority list, which is spent — everything on it was taken. What is worth keeping is why some of it cost more than it should have. Four lessons, all of which produced real defects.

Enumerate; do not estimate. Every colour and orphan finding I reported by survey was wrong. The purple was reported eliminated while a second variant sat on a contact marker. The dead bus keys were reported as four and were eight. The tally at the head of this document drifted from its own rows in three consecutive revisions. In every case the fix was to write a script that walked the file and diffed — every hex against the palette, every template hole against every emitted value, every row against the summary. That found four defects the eye had missed and has not produced a wrong count since.

A colour change scoped to the markup misses the logic class. The stencil lift was a string replace over the template, which left twenty-five state-dependent colours emitting pre-lift inks onto lightened grounds — a live contrast failure on six panels that survived for several revisions because the first audit read only the markup. Anything conditional on state lives in the logic and has to be read there.

Re-read the element before acting on a finding about it. Two rows were mis-stated when raised, and in both cases I acted on my own bad description rather than the thing itself. The top-left station cost four rejected attempts at a readout the board turned out not to need. Working rule: when a replacement takes more than two goes, question whether the part belongs at all.

A rule can be wrong. Three findings closed by amending the language document rather than the build: phosphor is a material and not an accent, ink value depends on the ground it sits on, and a channel selection is not a state. Two more closed by declining them — graticule labels stay on the glass because changing numbers communicate scale, and the plot does not magnify past its authored 1×. An audit that only ever changes the build will eventually enforce a rule that was never right.

09  DAMAGE AND ALERT BEHAVIOUR

Recorded here because these are rules, not visuals, and nothing else in the project states them. Two principles govern both: a compartment governs the system housed inside it, and a gated control names its own reason. Every gate below prints its cause on the panel that refuses.

COMPARTMENT INTEGRITY — 0 TO 5

A vented compartment counts as integrity zero whatever its structure, because nobody can crew it. That single rule means venting a compartment to fight a fire has the same operational cost as losing it, which is the decision the FLUSH order is meant to pose.

COMPARTMENTGOVERNSTHRESHOLD
RCTReactor units availableScales linearly: integrity 5 gives 10 units, 3 gives 6, 1 gives 2. Floored at 2 so AUX can always charge and a party can always be sent — a hit must never be unrecoverable.
MAGThe arming ladderBelow 3, LOAD refuses and reads MAGAZINE HIT. No rounds can be struck up.
HGRLaunchBelow 3, launch refuses — craft cannot be spotted in a wrecked bay.
BRIFiring solutionsBelow 3, COMPUTE returns nothing. The plot computer is on the bridge.
ENGUsable thrustScales the ENG bus ceiling linearly. At integrity 2, full allocation yields 40 percent thrust.
HABNothing directlyCarries the largest crew, so its loss dominates the complement figures.

ALERT CONDITION

Three states, cycled by the ALERT control. The condition is a permission gate, not a mood: it decides what the board will release and how fast damage control works.

CONDITIONCREW AT STATIONSRELEASES
3 — CRUISING42 percent of complementNothing. Weapons and flight ops both refuse and say so.
2 — ACTION74 percentWeapons and flight ops released. Repair at single rate.
1 — BATTLEFull complementEverything released. Damage-control parties closed up, repairing two integrity per order instead of one.

One consequence worth stating. The reactor cap is enforced on the tick, not only at allocation, so a core that loses capacity while buses are drawing sheds units from the lowest-priority bus until the total fits — AUX first, then sensors, then weapons, engines last. The operator sees bars drop without touching a dial, which is the correct read for a core that has just been hit.

The model below is specified, not built. R-10, R-11 and R-12 are one model, and the useful output is a specification the engine implements once rather than a JavaScript approximation that would have to be reverse-engineered later. Everything above this rule is live in the build; everything below it is intent.

09.4  ARMOUR AND PENETRATION

Four faces — port, starboard, bow, aft. Each carries a continuous value from 100% down to breached: no discrete plates, no hit-location roll within a face. While a face holds, the compartments behind it are untouched. Once it reaches zero the next hit on that face lands in a fixed compartment, deterministically rather than weighted, so the operator can learn which face protects what and turn the ship deliberately to present armour that still holds.

FACECOMPARTMENT BEHIND ITWHAT IS LOST
BOWMagazineRailgun feed, missile stores
AFTEngine roomThrust, and the ENG bus ceiling
PORTReactorUnits the core can make
STARBOARDHangar bayLaunch and recovery

Bridge and habitation sit inboard of all four and are reached only through a neighbouring compartment, so they are the last to suffer and the first to matter when they do.

09.5  INTERNAL CONDITIONS

A compartment carries an integrity value and, independently of it, any of three conditions. All three spread, each by its own route, so an unattended condition becomes a larger problem than the hit that caused it.

09.6  COMPONENTS

Components fail individually, inside a compartment that may still be intact — a railgun mount, an autoloader, a sensor array, a comms set. Two failure modes, and they differ in what the operator must do about them.

MODECAUSECLEARS
JAMMEDImpact, or a cycling fault under loadOnly by a damage-control party. Takes time and occupies crew.
OVERHEATEDSustained draw beyond the component’s ratingBy itself, once the bus is vented or the system idled. No crew needed.

The distinction is the point. Overheat is a consequence of how hard the operator is pushing and is reversible by easing off; a jam is damage and must be crewed. One teaches restraint, the other costs a party.

09.7  WHERE A FAULT SURFACES

Both places, saying different things. Engineering carries the diagnosis — which component, in which compartment, in which state. The system’s own panel carries the consequence: it refuses the action and states the reason on the control that is gated, in the same voice as every interlock already on the board. The operator never has to open engineering to find out why a shot will not fire.

09.8  STABILITY AS A DAMAGE SOURCE

Sustained G above a threshold accumulates structural damage over time — not a per-manoeuvre chance, and not confined to armour. Holding a hard turn degrades the hull itself, so stability becomes a budget the operator spends rather than a warning they can ignore. This also gives R-08 its mechanism: casualties follow from the same accumulation, which is what the HAZARD lamp already promises.

09.9  DAMAGE-CONTROL PARTIES

Repair is neither instant nor free. A party is dispatched to a compartment, takes time to arrive and to work, and occupies crew who leave their station while assigned — which ties damage control to alert condition, since a ship at Condition 1 has everyone at stations and fewer hands spare. A party can be recalled, and can be lost if the compartment vents or burns around it. Only a party can clear a jam or fight a fire.

09.10  WHAT THE BOARD STILL NEEDS

The model demands five things the board does not have. These stay design problems, not engine ones.

#NEEDEDNOTE

One caution on sequencing: these five would roughly double what engineering carries, and it is already the densest drawer on the board. NOTE ON THIS AUDIT’S OWN METHOD

The first pass of this document examined the markup and not the logic class, which is why it missed the half-migrated inks, the off-palette purple and the dead power values — all three live in computed state rather than in the template. That is the same blind spot that caused C-02 in the build. Any future audit of colour or of orphaned values has to read both halves of the component.
